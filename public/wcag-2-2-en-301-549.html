WCAG 2.2 is not yet EN 301 549

Plenty of tools will tell you that every WCAG 2.2 failure is an EN 301 549 gap. Six of them are not, because the version of the standard actually cited in the Official Journal restates WCAG 2.1. The difference is small, entirely knowable, and worth getting right if you are writing down what the law requires of you.

How the chain works

The European Accessibility Act does not name WCAG. It points at harmonised standards, and conformance with one gives you a presumption of conformity with the Act. For web content that standard is EN 301 549, whose clause 9 restates the WCAG success criteria as numbered requirements — WCAG 1.4.3 becomes clause 9.1.4.3, and so on.

So the question “which WCAG applies” is really the question “which version of EN 301 549 is cited”.

Which version is cited

Versions of EN 301 549 and the WCAG version each restates
VersionRestatesRequirementsStatus
v3.2.1WCAG 2.1 A/AA50Cited in the Official Journal — in force
v4.1.1WCAG 2.2 A/AA55Not yet cited — V4.1.0 draft, November 2025

v3.2.1 was published in March 2021. A newer version aligning clauses 9, 10 and 11 to WCAG 2.2 went out for public enquiry as draft V4.1.0 in November 2025. Until the final version is cited, it does not carry the presumption of conformity.

You will see October 2026 quoted as the expected citation date. Every source we could find for that is secondary — vendor commentary rather than an EU instrument — so treat it as a direction of travel and not a deadline.

The six criteria in the gap

WCAG 2.2 added six Level A and AA criteria. Each is a real barrier for real people, and none of them currently has a clause in EN 301 549:

CriterionNameLevel
2.4.11Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)AA
2.5.7Dragging MovementsAA

Target Size is the one automated tools actually catch, which is why it is the one most likely to be reported to you as an EN 301 549 failure. It is not one, yet.

The criterion that runs the other way

4.1.1 Parsing is in WCAG 2.1, so EN 301 549 v3.2.1 contains clause 9.4.1.1 — but WCAG 2.2 removed the criterion, and W3C annotated it in 2.0 and 2.1 with a note that it should be considered as always satisfied for any content using HTML or XML. Browsers and assistive technology stopped caring about markup validity in the way the criterion assumed.

In practice this means the clause exists and cannot be failed. Problems that used to be filed under it — a duplicate id breaking a label association, say — belong under 1.3.1 Info and Relationships or 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value. A tool still reporting 4.1.1 failures is reporting against a criterion W3C has retired.

So what should you build to?

WCAG 2.2. The alignment is coming, the six extra criteria address genuine barriers, and nobody has ever been penalised for exceeding a standard. The distinction matters for two narrower things: how you describe your obligations in writing, and how you prioritise a backlog when you cannot fix everything at once.

A failure of clause 9.1.4.3 is a failure of the standard the Act points at today. A failure of WCAG 2.5.8 is a barrier you should fix that is not yet part of that standard. Both are worth your time; only one of them is currently the thing a regulator would measure you against.

How this tool handles it

Our free check reports the two separately. Findings that map to a clause of the version in force are listed as requirement failures with their clause number. Findings against a WCAG 2.2 criterion with no clause yet are listed under their criterion number and marked as not yet required, with a note saying what they are waiting on. We never invent a clause number for a requirement that does not exist — which is easy to do by accident, since clause numbers are derived from criterion numbers.
