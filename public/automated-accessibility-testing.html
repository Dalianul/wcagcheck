Scope · what a machine can and cannot decide

What an automated check can and cannot prove

A scanner finds roughly a third of accessibility problems. That third is worth finding — it is cheap, repeatable and catches the failures that affect the most people. But a clean result is not compliance, and a tool that lets you believe otherwise has done you harm rather than good.

What a machine is genuinely good at

Anything that reduces to a rule about markup or computed style. Whether an image has an alt attribute. Whether a form control has a label. Whether a button has an accessible name. Whether the contrast ratio between two resolved colours clears 4.5:1. Whether an ARIA attribute is valid on the element carrying it. These are decidable, and a machine decides them faster and more consistently than any person.

What it structurally cannot see

The requirements that turn on meaning. A machine can tell you an image has alt text. It cannot tell you the alt text describes the image. It can tell you a heading exists; it cannot tell you the heading is honest about what follows. It can tell you an error message appeared; it cannot tell you the message explains how to fix the error.

Then there is everything that requires doing something rather than reading something: whether you can complete a purchase with a keyboard alone, whether focus order matches the visual order, whether a modal traps you, whether a video has an audio description that describes anything useful. Those need a person, and often a person using the assistive technology in question.

The specific limits of this tool

We run axe-core in your own browser against the page you name, and a separate pass over the raw HTML. Three limits are worth stating plainly:

  • Scripts on the scanned page never run. That is a security decision, not an oversight — executing arbitrary third-party JavaScript in our origin would be a hole in this site. Content built entirely by JavaScript is therefore invisible to the check.
  • Colour contrast is judged only for what fits on screen. Contrast needs real layout at real coordinates; content below the fold comes back unassessed rather than passing.
  • Some sites refuse us. Bot protection returns 403, 429 or 511 to automated requests. We say so rather than reporting the site as broken.

Why we list requirements we cannot check

Most scanners show you what failed and stop. That reads as a clean bill of health when the list is short, because nothing on the page distinguishes “we checked this and it passed” from “we never looked”.

Our report lists every requirement in clause 9 of EN 301 549, and marks the ones automation never examined as needing a person. On a typical page that is the majority of them. It makes the report look less flattering and considerably more useful, because the untested rows are your actual remaining work.

Results the engine could not decide get their own category too, and are never counted as passes. Contrast over a gradient is the common case: the honest answer is that it could not be determined, and saying so beats guessing in either direction.

Overlays do not close the gap

An accessibility overlay is JavaScript that adjusts a page after it loads. It cannot know what an image depicts, cannot restructure a form, and cannot fix a keyboard trap inside somebody else’s script. It also cannot remove the obligation, because what the law asks is that the service be accessible — not that a widget be installed on it. If a vendor promises compliance from a script tag, that promise is the product.

What to do with a scan result

Treat it as triage. Fix the requirement failures, because those are the machine-certain ones and they map to clauses of the standard. Look at the undecided rows next, since they are usually contrast problems that a person can settle in minutes. Then work through the untested requirements with a keyboard, a screen reader and, ideally, someone who uses one daily.
